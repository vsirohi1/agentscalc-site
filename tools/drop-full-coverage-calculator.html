Should I Drop Full Coverage? Calculator

Compare what you pay each year for collision and comprehensive with the most you could ever collect, and check the 10% rule.

How it works

Collision and comprehensive (together often called “full coverage”) pay to repair or replace your own car. As a car ages, its value drops but the premium often doesn’t. At some point you pay more to insure the car than the coverage could reasonably return. This calculator shows where your car stands.

Formula

Most you could collect = Car value − Deductible. Premium share = Annual collision + comprehensive premium ÷ Car value. Common rule: consider dropping when the share is above 10%.

How to use it

  1. Look up what the car would sell for today.
  2. Ask your insurer what the policy costs with and without collision and comprehensive; enter the difference.
  3. Enter your deductible.
  4. Say whether the car is financed or leased.

Worked example

A car worth $6,000 with a $1,000 deductible can pay out at most $5,000. If collision and comprehensive cost $900 a year, that is 15% of the car’s value — above the 10% rule — and about 5.6 years of premiums would add up to the most you could ever collect.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 10% rule?

A rule of thumb: if the yearly cost of collision and comprehensive is more than about 10% of the car’s value, consider dropping them — as long as you could afford to repair or replace the car yourself.

Can I drop full coverage on a financed car?

Usually not. Lenders and leasing companies require collision and comprehensive until the loan or lease ends.

Should I keep comprehensive and drop only collision?

It can make sense. Comprehensive (theft, hail, fire, hitting a deer) is often much cheaper than collision. Ask for each price separately.
