Actual Cash Value vs Replacement Cost: What Your Policy Will Really Pay

The difference between actual cash value and replacement cost, how depreciation and holdbacks work, and when replacement cost is worth paying for.

Two ways to pay a claim

Replacement cost pays what it costs to repair or replace damaged property with new property of similar kind and quality. Actual cash value (ACV) pays replacement cost minus depreciation — roughly what the item was worth just before the loss.

For a ten-year-old roof or a five-year-old TV, the difference can be large. An $18,000 roof that is seven years into a twenty-year life might be worth about $11,700 on an ACV basis — before the deductible.

How replacement cost is usually paid

Many replacement cost policies pay the ACV first, then pay the rest — the “recoverable depreciation” or holdback — once you have actually repaired or replaced the property and sent the receipts. There is usually a time limit for doing so.

Where ACV shows up

Some policies settle roofs on an ACV basis once they reach a certain age. Renters and condo policies may pay belongings at ACV unless you choose replacement cost. Federal flood policies pay contents at ACV. Car insurance pays the car’s actual cash value when it is totaled.

Is replacement cost worth it?

For belongings, replacement cost usually adds only a modest amount to the premium and can double what you receive for older items. For the home, ask how the roof is settled and whether the dwelling is written at replacement cost.
